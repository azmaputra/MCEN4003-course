How big is “big”?
Peak, peak-to-peak and RMS

Four ways to put a number on a vibration, and when each one misleads.

In Part B you described a vibration by how often it repeats and in what pattern. The other half of the story is how big it is, and that is less simple than it sounds.

Four ways to describe one sine wave

Vibration severity is judged from amplitude. But “amplitude” can mean several different numbers, even for one simple sine wave.

  • Peak (A): the largest excursion from zero. This matters when clearances or impacts are the concern.
  • Peak-to-peak (2A): the total swing. This is common for shaft displacement measured by proximity probes.
  • RMS (root mean square): a time-averaged level linked to vibration energy. This is the industry standard for velocity severity.
  • Average of the rectified signal (2A/π): rarely used today, but you will see it on older instruments.

Why RMS? Because real signals are not sine waves

A measured signal from a real machine contains many frequencies at once and never exactly repeats. For such a signal, a single peak reading depends on luck: one spike in a long record. What we need is a number that represents the average vibration energy over time. That number is the RMS.

Root-mean-square amplitude

$$y_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T y^2(t)\,dt} \tag{1.3}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • Square \(y^2\): makes every value positive, so the swings above and below zero no longer cancel. It also weights large values heavily.
  • Integrate \(\int_0^T\): adds up the squared values over the whole record length T.
  • Mean \(1/T\): turns that total into an average, the mean-square value.
  • Root \(\sqrt{\;}\): returns to the original units (mm/s, not mm²/s²).

Physical meaning: kinetic energy goes as velocity squared, so mean-square velocity is proportional to the average kinetic energy. RMS velocity is therefore a measure of how much energy the vibration carries.

Example 1.1

Using Eq. (1.3) by hand: how does the RMS of a signal compare with its peak?

The prediction above asked whether a random signal with peak 1.0 has an RMS of 0.707, less, or more. To answer it, you need to know where 0.707 comes from and what Eq. (1.3) actually does. Take a pen and paper: doing the arithmetic yourself is the quickest way to feel how the equation behaves.

(a) The exact result, using the integral in Eq. (1.3)

Start with a pure sine wave of peak A and apply Eq. (1.3) directly. Try each stage yourself before opening it.

Stage 1. What are we trying to find, and what do we start from?

We want \(y_{\text{rms}}\) for \(y(t) = A\sin\omega t\). We start from the RMS definition and integrate over one period \(T = 2\pi/\omega\):

$$y_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T A^2\sin^2\omega t\,dt} \tag{1.4}$$

Stage 2. Why use a trig identity here?

\(\sin^2\) is awkward to integrate directly. The identity \(\sin^2 x = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos 2x\) splits it into a constant plus an oscillating term, and each is easy to integrate:

$$y_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{A^2}{T}\int_0^T\left(\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos 2\omega t\right)dt} \tag{1.5}$$

Stage 3. What happens to each term?

The constant integrates to \(\tfrac12 T\). The cosine term integrates to \(\frac{\sin 2\omega T}{4\omega}\), and \(\sin(2\omega T) = \sin 4\pi = 0\). Over whole cycles, the oscillating part averages to zero:

$$y_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{A^2}{T}\cdot\frac{T}{2}} = \frac{A}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.707A \tag{1.6}$$

Stage 4. What does the result mean?

The mean-square of a sine wave is exactly half its peak squared, because sin² spends equal time above and below ½. The result depends on the shape of the signal, so RMS = peak/√2 is true only for a single pure sine wave.

(b) The same sine wave, digitised and calculated by hand

In practice, a digital analyser does not integrate a smooth curve. It samples the signal at equal time steps and applies the sampled form of Eq. (1.3): square every sample, add them up, divide by the number of samples N, then take the square root.

$$y_{\text{rms}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} y_n^2} \tag{1.7}$$

Squares: 0, 0.5, 1, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 1, 0.5. Sum = 4.0. Divide by N = 8: mean square = 0.5. Square root: yrms = √0.5 = 0.707, the same as the exact result in part (a).

So 0.707 is not a magic number: it comes from the sine wave spending a large share of its time close to its peak.

Answer to the prediction

The answer is B: less than 0.707. Squaring rewards large values, but one large sample is diluted by many small ones when you divide by N. A sine wave keeps returning close to its peak, so its RMS is high (0.707 × peak). A random signal reaches its peak only occasionally, so its RMS is a much smaller fraction of the peak. Crest factor: how spiky is the signal?

In Example 1.1, both signals had the same peak of 1.0, but very different RMS values: 0.707 for the sine wave and about 0.41 for the irregular signal. Turn this around. Two signals can have the same RMS and still have very different peaks. Engineers capture this difference with one number.

Crest factor

$$\text{CF} = \frac{y_{\text{peak}}}{y_{\text{rms}}} \tag{1.8}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • \(y_{\text{peak}}\) is the largest absolute value in the record. \(y_{\text{rms}}\) comes from Eq. (1.3), using the same record.
  • CF has no units, so it is the same whether you measure in mm/s or m/s².
  • CF is never less than 1, because a signal cannot have an RMS above its peak. It equals 1 only for a signal that stays at its peak all the time, such as a square wave.
  • From Example 1.1: the sine wave gives \(1/0.707 = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41\). The irregular signal gives \(1/0.41 \approx 2.4\).
  • A large CF means the vibration arrives in short, sharp bursts, with long quiet spells in between. This is the signature of impacts.

💡Engineering Intuition

RMS tells you how much vibration energy there is. Crest factor tells you how it is delivered: as a smooth push, or as a series of hammer blows. Two machines with the same RMS can treat their bearings very differently.

Learning question: How is RMS built up, and when does it hide something important?

Was your prediction correct? Why did the system behave this way?

For a random signal, RMS is usually well below peak/√2. The √2 shortcut only works for a pure sine wave.

A sine wave spends a lot of its time near its peak. A random signal reaches its peak only occasionally and spends most of its time at lower levels, so its RMS is a smaller fraction of the peak. The ratio peak/RMS is the crest factor. It is √2 ≈ 1.41 for a sine wave, typically 3 or more for random vibration, and much higher for impulsive signals. In the impulsive case, raising the impact strength ten-fold multiplied the peak by about seven but the RMS by only about four.

RMS in industry: trending and severity limits

In condition monitoring, the RMS velocity is often called the overall value. It is measured at the same points each month and plotted as a trend. A flat trend means a stable machine; a rising trend is a call to investigate. Severity standards then give alarm limits that depend on machine size and foundation type.

⚠Common Misconception: “The RMS is in the safe zone, so the machine is healthy”

RMS averages over time, so a short, sharp impact barely moves it. A bearing with a cracked race produces brief spikes once per ball pass: the peak and crest factor jump, while the RMS can stay comfortably inside the green zone. The impacts matter far more than the RMS value suggests.

What to do instead: also trend peak or crest factor, and look at the time waveform and spectrum. Module 9 covers bearing faults in detail.

The role of crest factor in condition monitoring

Crest factor is most useful for rolling-element bearings. A small defect, such as a spall on the outer race, is struck by every ball that rolls over it. Each strike is a short impact. Trending RMS and crest factor together shows how the damage develops.

  1. Healthy. The vibration is low and random-like. RMS and crest factor are both steady.
  2. Early defect. Short impacts appear. Each one raises the peak, but they are too brief to change the average. The crest factor rises while the RMS stays flat. This is the earliest warning.
  3. Damage spreads. The defect grows, the impacts become more frequent, and the gaps between them fill in. The RMS starts to climb and the crest factor stops rising.
  4. Near failure. The RMS is high. The crest factor falls back, sometimes close to its healthy value, because the RMS has caught up with the peak.

⚠Common Misconception: “The crest factor has dropped, so the bearing is getting better”

Late in a bearing’s life, the crest factor falls because the RMS has risen to meet the peak, not because the impacts have gone. Never judge crest factor on its own. Read it alongside the RMS trend: a falling crest factor with a rising RMS is a sign of advanced damage.

Types of signals

Each signal below is plotted as a function of time, so we say it is in the time domain. In measurement, this plot is called the time waveform. Q1. A pure sinusoidal velocity has a peak of 10 mm/s. What is its RMS?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. 10 mm/s

✗ Not quite. That is the peak. RMS is an average over the cycle, so it must be lower.

B. 7.07 mm/s

✓ Correct. For a pure sine wave, RMS = peak/√2 = 10/1.414 = 7.07 mm/s.

C. 6.37 mm/s

✗ Not quite. That is the rectified average, 2A/π. It is a different quantity from RMS.

D. 20 mm/s

✗ Not quite. That is peak-to-peak, the full swing from minimum to maximum.

Q2. Spot the error: “The random vibration on our compressor peaked at 14 mm/s, so its RMS is 14/√2 = 9.9 mm/s. That is above our limit.”

Compare with the model answer

The √2 rule holds only for a single pure sine wave. A random signal usually has a crest factor of 3 or more, so its RMS is likely around 3–5 mm/s, not 9.9. The RMS must be computed from the whole signal, which is what the data collector does. Using peak/√2 here would trigger a false alarm.

Q3. Which signal type would most likely give a high crest factor but a modest RMS?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. Harmonic

✗ Not quite. A pure sine wave has a crest factor of only √2 ≈ 1.41, far lower than an impulsive signal.

B. Impulsive

✓ Correct. Short, sharp spikes create large peaks but contribute little to the time average. That is why RMS alone can miss early bearing damage.

C. Pulsating

✗ Not quite. A pulsating signal, made of two nearby frequencies, has a peak close to the sum of the two amplitudes, giving a crest factor of about 2, which is not unusually high.

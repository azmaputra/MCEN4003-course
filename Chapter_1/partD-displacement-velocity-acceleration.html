Displacement, velocity or acceleration?

Three views of the same motion, and how to choose what to measure.

In Part C, every severity limit was quoted in mm/s RMS, which is a velocity. Yet many sensors measure displacement or acceleration instead. For one harmonic vibration, how are the three related, and which one should you measure?

In practice, most machine vibration is measured with an accelerometer on the bearing housing or casing. The analyser then integrates the signal: once to give velocity, and twice to give displacement. So you can choose which quantity to look at after the measurement. For a harmonic vibration, all three describe the same motion, but they weight frequency very differently.

The equations, one derivative at a time

Displacement: where we start

$$y(t) = A\sin\omega t, \qquad y_{pk} = A \tag{1.9}$$

Velocity: differentiate once. Why does ω appear?

$$v(t) = \frac{dy}{dt} = A\omega\cos\omega t, \qquad v_{pk} = A\omega = \omega\,y_{pk} \tag{1.10}$$

Covering the same distance A in a shorter time (higher ω) needs a higher speed. Each derivative brings out one factor of ω.

Acceleration: differentiate again. Why the minus sign?

$$a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = -A\omega^2\sin\omega t, \qquad a_{pk} = A\omega^2 = \omega^2 y_{pk} \tag{1.11}$$

The minus sign means acceleration always points back toward equilibrium, opposite to displacement. That is the spring's restoring force at work. Displacement and acceleration are 180° out of phase.

Linking the three amplitudes

$$v_{pk} = \omega\,y_{pk}, \qquad a_{pk} = \omega^2\,y_{pk}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f \tag{1.12}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • At low frequency, displacement is large while acceleration is tiny. Use displacement or velocity.
  • At high frequency, displacement becomes microscopic while acceleration is large. Use acceleration.
  • Velocity sits in the middle and stays relatively flat across the 10–1,000 Hz range where most machines run. This is one reason severity standards use mm/s RMS.

Engineering significance: choose the quantity that matches the frequency range and the failure mechanism you care about.

Amplitude conventions in industry

Each quantity has its own customary way of being reported. Displacement is usually quoted peak-to-peak, because it is compared with clearances such as a seal gap or a bearing clearance. Velocity is quoted as RMS, because the severity standards in Part C are written that way. Acceleration is quoted in g, as RMS or peak depending on the instrument. Mixing these up is a common source of error: the same vibration differs by a factor of √2 between peak and RMS, and 2√2 ≈ 2.83 between peak-to-peak and RMS. So always state the quantity, the unit and the measure, for example “22 µm pk-pk” or “1.0 mm/s RMS”.

Displacement, velocity and acceleration

Learning question: For one harmonic vibration, how do the three amplitude measures trade off as frequency changes?

Was your prediction correct? Why did the system behave this way?

At the same 1 mm/s RMS, the 5 Hz fan moves 400 times further than the 2,000 Hz gearbox: about 90 µm pk-pk versus about 0.23 µm.

Displacement per cycle is velocity divided by ω. With 400 times the frequency, the gearbox has 1/400 of the time per cycle, so it travels 1/400 of the distance. Its acceleration, however, is 400 times larger. This is why an accelerometer is the natural choice for high-frequency defects. Proximity probes do a different job: they watch the shaft inside a journal bearing (see “Which sensor?” below).

Industry uses ready-made conversions in mixed units. ISO 14694 gives them with frequency f in CPM, D in µm pk-pk, V in mm/s RMS and A in g RMS:

Unit conversion formulas (ISO 14694)

$$D = \frac{27\,009\,V}{f}, \qquad V = \frac{93\,712\,A}{f}, \qquad D = \frac{2.53\times10^{9}\,A}{f^{2}} \tag{1.13}$$

What does this equation tell us?

These are the same \(v = \omega y\) and \(a = \omega y\cdot\omega\) relations, with every unit conversion folded into the constant. Example 1.2 below shows where 27,009 comes from, so you never need to treat it as magic.

Example 1.2

Where does 27,009 come from? Deriving \(D = 27{,}009\,V\!/\!f\)

The constant 27,009 in Eq. (1.13) looks as if it came from nowhere. It is simply \(v_{pk} = \omega\,y_{pk}\) with every unit conversion folded in. Work through each stage on paper before opening it.

Stage 1. What physical principle do we start from?

The link between peak velocity and peak displacement for a harmonic vibration, from Eq. (1.12): \(v_{pk} = \omega\,y_{pk}\). This only holds in consistent SI units, so every quantity must first be converted.

Stage 2. Convert each quantity to SI peak values

\(v_{pk} = \sqrt2\,V\times10^{-3}\) m/s, because V is RMS in mm/s.

\(y_{pk} = (D/2)\times10^{-6}\) m, because D is peak-to-peak in µm.

\(\omega = 2\pi f/60\) rad/s, because f is in CPM.

Stage 3. Substitute into the principle

$$\sqrt2\,V\times10^{-3} = \frac{D}{2}\times10^{-6}\times\frac{2\pi f}{60} \tag{1.14}$$

Stage 4. Rearrange for D and evaluate the constant

$$D = \frac{2\sqrt2\times60\times10^{3}}{2\pi}\,\frac{V}{f} = \frac{27\,009\,V}{f} \tag{1.15}$$

Check your working: 2√2 × 60 × 10³ = 169,706, and 169,706 ÷ 2π = 27,009.

Engineering interpretation

Every “strange” constant in an engineering formula is just unit conversion. Being able to rebuild it protects you from using it with the wrong units, for example Hz instead of CPM, or peak instead of RMS.

Example 1.3

Interpreting a single velocity reading

Engineering problem

A data collector reports that a fan's vibration is dominated by one component at 1,200 CPM with an amplitude of 1.0 mm/s RMS. The maintenance planner asks what this means in displacement and acceleration terms.

Given

  • Frequency f = 1,200 CPM (single harmonic component)
  • Velocity V = 1.0 mm/s RMS

Before calculating, predict

Is the displacement likely to be closer to 1 µm, 20 µm or 1 mm? Write down your guess first.

Solution (open one step at a time)

Step 1: Convert the frequency

\(f = 1200/60 = 20\) Hz, so \(\omega = 2\pi\times20 = 125.7\) rad/s.

Step 2: Displacement

\(D = 27\,009\times1.0/1200 = 22.5\) µm pk-pk. A chart reading of about 23 µm agrees.

First-principles check: \(v_{pk} = 1.414\) mm/s, so \(y_{pk} = 1.414/125.7 = 0.01125\) mm = 11.25 µm, and pk-pk = 22.5 µm ✓.

Step 3: Acceleration

\(A = 1.0\times1200/93\,712 = 0.0128\) g RMS.

In SI units: \(0.0128\times9.81 = 0.126\) m/s² RMS, which is 126 mm/s². Check: \(a_{rms} = \omega\,v_{rms} = 125.7\times1.0 = 126\) mm/s² ✓.

Engineering interpretation

Open after you finish the calculation

A displacement of 22 µm is about a third of the thickness of a human hair, yet 1 mm/s RMS is a perfectly ordinary, healthy reading for a fan. The acceleration of 0.013 g is tiny. Notice how different the three numbers look although they describe one motion. Always state the quantity and the unit: peak, peak-to-peak or RMS.

Engineering decision: what should the engineer do next?

Think it through, then compare

At 1.0 mm/s RMS, this machine sits in zone A/B for most machine groups. No action is needed beyond continuing to trend it. For a 20 Hz component, velocity is the right quantity to trend. If the planner were worried about rubbing seals, displacement would be the better quantity to discuss.

⚠Common Misconception: “Small displacement means small vibration”

A displacement of 5 µm peak sounds tiny. At 5 kHz, it corresponds to roughly 500 g of peak acceleration, enough to destroy a bearing quickly. “Small” and “large” only make sense together with a frequency and the failure mechanism you care about.

Which sensor? Measuring the casing or the shaft

The quantity you want to see does not decide the sensor on its own. What decides it is what you want to see move.

Casing vibration (absolute). An accelerometer is stud-mounted on the bearing housing and measures how the housing moves in space. This is the standard sensor for portable data collection and for most online monitoring. The analyser integrates the acceleration once to give velocity, the mm/s RMS overall value from Part C, and twice to give displacement.

Shaft vibration (relative). In machines with fluid-film (journal) bearings, such as steam turbines, compressors and large pumps, the shaft floats on a film of oil. The oil film absorbs much of the shaft’s motion, so the casing moves far less than the rotor does. Non-contact proximity probes (eddy-current probes) are mounted through the bearing housing and measure the gap to the shaft. This gives the shaft’s displacement relative to the bearing. Two probes, 90° apart, track the path the shaft centre traces out.

⚠Common Misconception: “To see displacement, you need a displacement sensor”

Most displacement readings taken on a casing come from an accelerometer whose signal the analyser has integrated twice. Integration has a limit, though. Each step divides by ω, so very low frequencies, including low-frequency noise, are strongly amplified. Analysers apply a high-pass filter to stop this, and below a few hertz the integrated displacement becomes unreliable.

For a slow machine, such as the cooling-tower fan above running at a few hertz, engineers therefore use a low-frequency (high-sensitivity) accelerometer or a velocity sensor. A proximity probe is chosen for a different reason: it sees the shaft, not the casing.

✔CHECKPOINT — Can you explain this?

Answer in your head or on paper first. Then open the feedback.

Q1. At the same velocity of 5 mm/s RMS, how does the displacement at 10 Hz compare with that at 1,000 Hz?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. It is the same.

✗ Not quite. Displacement = velocity/ω. A hundred times the frequency means a hundredth of the displacement.

B. It is 100 times larger at 10 Hz.

✓ Correct. y = v/ω, so displacement scales as 1/f. In the opposite direction, acceleration (a = ωv) is 100 times larger at 1,000 Hz.

C. It is 10,000 times larger at 10 Hz.

✗ Not quite. That would be the ratio for constant acceleration, where y = a/ω² scales as 1/f². Here velocity is held constant.

Q2. You need to detect early bearing defects that show up at around 5 kHz. Which measurement do you choose?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. A proximity probe measuring displacement

✗ Not quite. A proximity probe watches the shaft inside a journal bearing, not the bearing housing, where rolling-element bearing defects show up. And at 5 kHz the displacement is tiny, so it would be lost in the noise.

B. An accelerometer measuring acceleration in g

✓ Correct. Acceleration grows with f², so high-frequency impacts produce strong acceleration signals. This is standard practice for bearing monitoring.

C. Overall velocity RMS only

✗ Not quite. Velocity is good for general severity, but it de-emphasises high frequencies. The RMS averaging also hides impacts, as you saw in Part C.

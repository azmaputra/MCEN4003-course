Free vibration, or what happens when you let go

Natural frequency, damping, and how to measure damping from a decay.

Part E turned a real system into a mass, a spring and a damper. Now we ask what that model does when you disturb it once and then leave it alone. This is where we finally answer the question from Part A: what is the skid's ring telling us?

Pluck a guitar string, strike a tuning fork or tap a pump skid with a mallet. Each one vibrates at its own frequency, set by the structure itself rather than by how hard you hit it, and then slowly dies away. This is free vibration: motion after an initial disturbance, with no continuing force.

Step 1: The equation of motion

Return to the model from the predict activity: a mass m on a spring k, with no damper. Measure x downward from static equilibrium, the same direction as the trace in the animation.

Each stage below answers a question. Try each one yourself before opening it.

What are we trying to find, and what principle do we use?

We want x(t), the motion of the mass after it is released. The principle is Newton's second law, applied to the mass on its own:

$$\sum F = m\ddot{x}$$

with forces taken positive in the same direction as x, downward.

At rest in position (b), what balances the weight?

The mass is at rest, so nothing is accelerating. Start from static equilibrium:

$$\sum F = 0$$

Two forces act: the weight mg downward (positive) and the spring force kδst upward (negative), because the spring is compressed by δst:

$$mg - k\,\delta_{st} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad k\,\delta_{st} = mg$$

Keep this result. You will need it in two stages’ time.

Displaced by x in position (c), which forces act on the mass?

Cut the mass free from the spring and draw every force on it:

  • Weight mg, downward: positive.
  • Spring force k(δst + x), upward: negative.

Newton's second law, adding the forces with their signs:

$$mg - k(\delta_{st} + x) = m\ddot{x}$$

Where did gravity go?

Expand the bracket: \(mg - k\delta_{st} - kx = m\ddot{x}\). From position (b), \(k\delta_{st} = mg\), so the first two terms cancel:

$$-kx = m\ddot{x}$$

The weight is still there, but it is exactly carried by the static compression of the spring. Measuring x from static equilibrium removes both from the equation. The minus sign is the restoring force: push the mass down and the net spring force pushes it up; lift it and the net force pulls it down.

What is the resulting equation, and what does it mean?

$$m\ddot{x} + kx = 0 \tag{1.19}$$

Inertia force plus spring force equals zero, because nothing is driving the system after release. It is “free”. With no damper, nothing removes energy, so the motion never dies away. That is exactly what the predict animation showed.

Real systems have damping, and you will add it in Step 3. In Module 2, the zero on the right becomes a driving force F(t).

💡Engineering Intuition: rotation follows the same rules

A disc twisting on a shaft vibrates for the same reason a mass bounces on a spring. Swap each translational quantity for its rotational partner and the whole derivation carries over unchanged:

Translational and rotational free vibration compared
TranslationalRotational
Displacement\(x\) (m)\(\theta\) (rad)
Inertiamass \(m\) (kg)mass moment of inertia \(J_0\) (kg·m²)
Stiffness\(k\) (N/m)torsional stiffness \(k_t\) (N·m/rad)
Restoring actionforce \(kx\)torque \(k_t\theta\)
Equation of motion\(m\ddot{x} + kx = 0\)\(J_0\ddot{\theta} + k_t\theta = 0\)
Natural frequency\(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}\)\(\omega_n = \sqrt{k_t/J_0}\)

A heavier or larger disc (bigger \(J_0\)) twists back more slowly. A shorter or thicker shaft (bigger \(k_t\)) snaps back faster. For a shaft, \(k_t = GJ/L\) from the stiffness table in Part E.

Watch the symbols: in \(GJ/L\), J is the polar second moment of area of the shaft cross-section (m⁴). \(J_0\) here is the mass moment of inertia of the disc (kg·m²). Same letter, different quantity.

Step 2: The natural frequency

Before the formula, the intuition. A stiffer spring pulls back harder, so the mass returns more quickly and the natural frequency rises. A heavier mass responds more sluggishly to the same pull, so the natural frequency falls.

Now the mathematics. Start from Eq. (1.19) and open each stage in turn.

What are we looking for, and what solution do we try?

We want the frequency at which the system oscillates on its own. The predict animation showed a smooth, repeating motion after release, so try a harmonic solution with an unknown amplitude X and an unknown frequency ω:

$$x = X\sin\omega t$$

What are the velocity and acceleration?

Differentiate twice, as in Part D:

$$\dot{x} = \omega X\cos\omega t, \qquad \ddot{x} = -\omega^2 X\sin\omega t$$

The acceleration is the displacement multiplied by \(-\omega^2\): it always points back towards equilibrium.

Substitute into Eq. (1.19). What do we get?

$$m\left(-\omega^2 X\sin\omega t\right) + k\left(X\sin\omega t\right) = 0$$

Collect the common factor:

$$\left(k - m\omega^2\right) X\sin\omega t = 0$$

Which frequency makes this true at every instant?

The equation must hold for all t. \(X\sin\omega t\) is not zero at every instant, and \(X = 0\) would mean no motion at all. So the bracket must vanish:

$$k - m\omega^2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega^2 = \frac{k}{m}$$

Only one frequency works. It is set by k and m alone, not by X. This is the natural frequency \(\omega_n\). Divide by 2π to convert from rad/s to Hz.

The result:

Undamped natural frequency

$$\begin{aligned} \omega_n &= \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} &&\quad \text{(rad/s)} \\[6pt] f_n &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} &&\quad \text{(Hz)} \end{aligned} \tag{1.20}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • Increase k and fn rises, but only as √k. Doubling stiffness raises fn by 41%.
  • Increase m and fn falls as 1/√m. Doubling mass lowers fn to 71% of its value, so the period grows by 41%.
  • fn does not depend on how hard you hit the system. Amplitude and frequency are independent, as in Part B.

Engineering significance: fn is a property of the structure, and k and m are the only levers. You will use exactly these levers to move natural frequencies away from running speeds.

A useful field shortcut: under its own weight, a mass on a spring sags by \(\delta_{st} = mg/k\). Substituting \(k/m = g/\delta_{st}\):

Natural frequency from static deflection

$$f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta_{st}}} \;\approx\; \frac{15.8}{\sqrt{\delta_{st}\,[\text{mm}]}}\ \text{Hz} \tag{1.21}$$

What does this equation tell us?

Measure how far the mounts compress when the machine is installed, and you have its bounce natural frequency without knowing k or m. A 1 mm sag gives about 16 Hz, and a 25 mm sag gives about 3 Hz. Soft isolators work by giving a low fn, as you will see in Module 3.

⚠Common Misconception: “Natural frequency and forcing frequency are the same thing”

The natural frequency fn belongs to the structure: it is what the skid rings at when struck. The forcing frequency belongs to the source: for example, 1× pump speed. They are independent. The trouble starts when they are close, which is the subject of Module 2.

Step 3: Damping and the damping ratio

Ride a scooter briskly up a short incline, such as a driveway ramp. At the top, the suspension springs let the body bounce, but the bouncing does not go on forever. Watch how quickly it dies away.

The springs alone would keep the body bouncing, just like the undamped valve in the predict activity. It is the shock absorbers that take energy out of each bounce and turn it into heat. That is the job of damping.

Now put the damper back into the model. A viscous damper resists the velocity, so the free-body diagram gains one more upward force, \(c\dot{x}\). Eq. (1.19) becomes:

$$m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0 \tag{1.22}$$

Inertia plus damping plus stiffness equals zero. Each term is one element of the model.

Damping decides what happens after release. The key number is the damping ratio ζ. It compares the actual damping c with the critical damping cc, the smallest damping that just prevents oscillation.

Critical damping and damping ratio

$$\begin{aligned} c_c &= 2\sqrt{km} = 2m\omega_n &&\quad \text{(N·s/m)} \\[6pt] \zeta &= \frac{c}{c_c} &&\quad \text{(dimensionless)} \end{aligned} \tag{1.23}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • ζ < 1, underdamped: the system oscillates while decaying. Almost every engineering structure is in this range.
  • ζ = 1, critically damped: the fastest return to equilibrium without overshoot.
  • ζ > 1, overdamped: there is no oscillation, and the return is slower than the critical case.

Because cc grows with √(km), the same damper gives a smaller ζ on a heavier or stiffer system.

For the underdamped case, the solution is a sine wave inside a decaying exponential envelope:

Underdamped free response†

$$\begin{aligned} x(t) &= X e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi) &&\quad \text{(m)} \\[6pt] \omega_d &= \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} &&\quad \text{(rad/s)} \end{aligned} \tag{1.24}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • \(e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\): the envelope. A larger \(\zeta\omega_n\) gives a faster decay.
  • \(\omega_d\): the damped natural frequency, the frequency you actually observe.
  • X and \(\phi\): set by the initial conditions, meaning how the system was released or struck.

For ζ = 0.05, ωd is 99.9% of ωn. In practice, light damping changes how long the ringing lasts, not its frequency.

† For the full derivation of Eq. (1.24), see D. J. Inman, Engineering Vibration (Pearson).

Was your prediction correct? Why did the system behave this way?

Doubling the mass increases the period by √2 ≈ 1.41, not by 2.

The natural frequency goes as √(k/m), so twice the mass gives 1/√2 of the frequency and √2 times the period. Raising c past cc = 2√(km) removes the overshoot. With the same damper on a heavier mass, ζ falls, because cc rises with √m. With light damping, ωd and ωn differ by less than 1%. Damping mainly controls the decay, not the frequency.

Halving the branch length raises the frequency by √8 ≈ 2.8, not by 2 or 8. The cantilever stiffness is k = 3EI/L3, so a branch half as long is 23 = 8 times stiffer, and the frequency rises by √8. Length is a far stronger lever than mass.

Step 4: Measuring damping from a decay

Look again at Eq. (1.24). The oscillation sits inside the envelope \(Xe^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\), and the damping ratio ζ is in the exponent. The faster the peaks shrink, the larger ζ. So if we record a decay, we can read ζ straight from it.

This matters because you cannot read c off a drawing. Damping comes from joints, friction and materials, so engineers measure it. Strike the structure, record the decay, and compare two successive peaks one damped period \(T_d\) apart:

$$\frac{x_1}{x_2} = \frac{Xe^{-\zeta\omega_n t_1}}{Xe^{-\zeta\omega_n (t_1 + T_d)}} = e^{\zeta\omega_n T_d}$$

X and the sine term cancel, because the peaks are one full cycle apart. Only the damping is left. Taking the natural log gives the logarithmic decrement in Eq. (1.25).

Logarithmic decrement

$$\begin{aligned} \delta &= \ln\frac{x_1}{x_2} = \frac{2\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \approx 2\pi\zeta &&\quad \text{(dimensionless)} \\[6pt] \zeta &\approx \frac{\delta}{2\pi} &&\quad \text{(for small } \zeta) \end{aligned} \tag{1.25}$$

What does this equation tell us?

δ is the logarithmic decrement. The approximation is accurate for ζ < 0.2. For better accuracy with noisy data, use peaks n cycles apart: δ = (1/n) ln(x0/xn).

Typical orders of magnitude: welded steel structures ζ ≈ 0.01–0.02; bolted steel ≈ 0.02–0.05; reinforced concrete ≈ 0.02–0.05; elastomer mounts ≈ 0.05–0.15. A rough rule: the amplitude halves in about 0.11/ζ cycles.

From a measurement to a material property. The same test is how engineers rate damping materials. Test a steel strip bare, then bond a damping layer to it and test again. The rise in ζ is what the layer adds. Damping sheets rated this way are bonded to car floor pans, doors and bulkheads, to ship decks and to machine guards, to reduce panel vibration and noise.

Standard material tests, such as ASTM E756, use a similar coated cantilever but usually extract the damping from the frequency response instead of a decay. Material data sheets often quote the loss factor η; for light damping, η ≈ 2ζ.

⚠Common Misconception: “More damping is always better, and critical damping is ideal”

Critical damping is the fastest return without overshoot, but it is rarely the design target. Vehicle suspensions use roughly ζ ≈ 0.2–0.4 as a compromise between comfort and control. Many measuring instruments use about 0.6–0.7. Large damping also has costs: heat, wear and, as Module 3 shows, worse vibration isolation at high frequency.

Modelling a real system with mass, spring and damper

Turning a real machine into a model you can write equations for.

Parts B to D gave you the language for describing a vibration: how often, how big and in which quantity. That describes what a sensor sees. It does not explain why a structure responds the way it does, such as why the Pilbara skid rang when it was struck. For that, we need a model of the structure.

From machine to model

You cannot write an equation for “a pump on a skid”. You can write one for a model of it. A lumped-parameter or mass–spring–damper (MSD) model collapses a real system into three idealised elements:

  • Mass m stores kinetic energy. It resists changes in velocity.
  • Spring k stores potential energy. It pulls back toward equilibrium in proportion to displacement.
  • Damper c dissipates energy as heat. It resists motion in proportion to velocity.

💡Engineering Intuition

Vibration is energy sloshing back and forth between the mass (kinetic) and the spring (potential), with the damper removing a little on every cycle. Anything that adds energy at the right moment makes it grow; anything that removes energy makes it decay.

How many degrees of freedom do you need?

The number of degrees of freedom (DOF) is the number of independent coordinates needed to describe the motion. The right model is the simplest one that answers your engineering question.

Up next · Part F

Free vibration, or what happens when you let go

You now have a mass, a spring and a damper. Displace that model and let go, and it vibrates at a frequency of its own choosing: its natural frequency. This is the most important property of any vibrating system, because a machine running at or near it drives the structure into resonance. In Part F you will find out what sets the natural frequency, and how engineers keep operating speeds well away from it.

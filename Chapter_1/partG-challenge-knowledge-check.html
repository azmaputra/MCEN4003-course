ApplyEngineering challenge

Back to the Pilbara pump

The cooling-water pump from Part A has a 55 kW motor at 1,480 rpm, mounted on a steel skid bolted to a concrete plinth (ISO Group 2, rigid foundation). You are given the following data.

  • Overall velocity trend at the motor’s outboard bearing, vertical: 1.9, 2.6, 3.4, then 4.3 mm/s RMS over four months.
  • Spectrum: one dominant peak at 24.7 Hz, the 1× running speed. Harmonics are small.
  • Phase at 24.7 Hz between the skid’s front and rear corners: about 175°.
  • Bump test on the skid with the pump off: it rings at 26.0 Hz. Two successive peaks read 10.0 and 7.3 mm/s.
  • Commissioning records from two years ago: the skid’s bump test rang at 31 Hz.

Work through the tasks below as an engineer would. Write your reasoning before opening each expert comparison. There is no single numerical answer to find.

1. What is physically vibrating, and in what pattern?

Compare with an expert's reasoning

The whole skid is moving at 1× running speed. A phase of about 175° between the front and rear corners means it is rocking, not bouncing. The spectrum suggests the source is at the rotor frequency, but the structure is clearly involved.

Why 175° and not exactly 180°? Measured phase is never perfect. Sensor mounting, signal noise, small differences in local stiffness at each corner, and a little bouncing mixed in with the rocking all shift it slightly. In practice, a phase within 180° ± 20° is read as out of phase (rocking), and within 0° ± 20° as in phase (bouncing together).

2. How severe is it? Which ISO zone is it in, and how fast is it changing?

Compare with an expert's reasoning

4.3 mm/s RMS falls in zone C (2.8–4.5 mm/s) for Group 2 on a rigid foundation: acceptable only for a limited period. The trend is accelerating, so it will enter zone D (above 4.5 mm/s) at the next reading or soon after. This is urgent.

3. Estimate the skid's damping ratio from the bump test.

Compare with an expert's reasoning

δ = ln(10.0/7.3) = 0.315, so ζ ≈ δ/2π ≈ 0.050. That is typical of a bolted steel structure: lightly damped, so it amplifies strongly near resonance.

4. Why did the problem appear only recently? Use fn = (1/2π)√(k/m).

Compare with an expert's reasoning

fn fell from 31 Hz to 26 Hz. The mass has not changed, so stiffness must have dropped by (26/31)² = 0.70, a 30% loss of stiffness. Likely causes include loosened hold-down bolts, cracked or deteriorated grout, or corrosion and cracking at welds. As k fell, fn drifted toward the 24.7 Hz running speed (now only 5% away), so the same small unbalance force produces ever-larger motion. That explains a rising trend with no change to the pump.

5. What additional measurements would you request?

Compare with an expert's reasoning

Check bolt torque and grout condition, and measure the phase and amplitude at each skid foot and on the plinth to locate the soft corner. Repeat bump tests on the skid and plinth separately. Do a run-up or coast-down test to see the response peak as speed sweeps through fn. An operating deflection shape (Module 8) would show the motion directly. Also check the rotor balance, since unbalance supplies the force even if the structure is the amplifier.

6. What modifications would reduce the vibration, and what should you avoid?

Compare with an expert's reasoning

First, restore the stiffness: re-torque or replace the anchors and regrout. This should return fn toward 31 Hz. Better still, stiffen the skid so fn sits at least 20% above 24.7 Hz, which means above about 30 Hz with margin. Balance the rotor to reduce the force itself. Avoid adding mass: it lowers fn further toward running speed. Do not simply raise the alarm limit.

💡Engineering Intuition

The pump was the source, but the skid was the amplifier. Many “machine” vibration problems are really structural problems. Always ask both questions: what is pushing, and what is responding?

CheckKnowledge check

Five questions covering the whole module. Aim to answer them without your notes.

Q1 (conceptual). Sensors on the two feet of a motor show equal amplitude at 1× running speed, with a phase difference of 180°. Which description fits?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. The motor is bouncing vertically.

✗ Not quite. Bouncing means both feet move together, at Δφ ≈ 0°.

B. The motor is rocking about a point between the feet.

✓ Correct. Opposite motion at equal amplitude means rotation about the midpoint.

C. The vibration has cancelled out, so the motor is still.

✗ Not quite. The signals come from two different locations. Both feet really move; they just move in opposite directions.

Q2 (calculation). A pump runs at 2,970 rpm with a 1× velocity of 4.5 mm/s RMS. What is the displacement?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. About 41 µm pk-pk

✓ Correct. D = 27,009 × 4.5/2,970 = 40.9 µm pk-pk.

B. About 20 µm pk-pk

✗ Not quite. That is the peak value, half of peak-to-peak. Check which form the formula gives.

C. About 0.41 µm pk-pk

✗ Not quite. Check the formula: D = 27,009 V/f with f in CPM, not D = V/f.

D. About 2,450 µm pk-pk

✗ Not quite. Frequency must be in CPM. Using Hz (49.5) would give this wrong result.

Q3 (graph interpretation). A bearing’s monthly trend shows its crest factor rising from 3 to 6 over three months, then falling back to 3.5, while its overall RMS doubles over the same period. What is the most likely state of the bearing?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. The defect has healed: the crest factor is back near its healthy value.

✗ Not quite. Bearing damage does not heal. The crest factor fell because the RMS rose to meet the peak, not because the impacts stopped.

B. The damage has spread and is now advanced.

✓ Correct. An early rise in crest factor with a flat RMS is a local defect producing impacts. A falling crest factor with a rising RMS means the impacts have become frequent enough to lift the average. Read the two trends together.

C. The sensor is faulty, because the two trends disagree.

✗ Not quite. The trends do not disagree. This is the normal pattern of a failing rolling-element bearing, as shown in Part C.

Q4 (physical interpretation). A bump test shows successive peaks of 8.0 and 5.0 mm/s. What is the approximate damping ratio?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. 0.075

✓ Correct. δ = ln(8/5) = 0.47, and ζ ≈ 0.47/2π = 0.075.

B. 0.47

✗ Not quite. 0.47 is the logarithmic decrement δ. Divide by 2π to get ζ.

C. 0.375

✗ Not quite. That is (8 − 5)/8, a simple percentage drop. Damping uses the logarithm of the ratio.

Q5 (diagnosis). A gearbox's overall velocity is 2.1 mm/s RMS, comfortably in zone B, but technicians report an intermittent knocking sound. What is the best next step?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. Nothing. The RMS is within limits.

✗ Not quite. RMS averages over time and can hide short impacts. The knocking is a warning sign.

B. Look at the time waveform, peak and crest factor,
and acceleration at higher frequency.

✓ Correct. Impacts raise peak and crest factor and show in acceleration long before RMS velocity moves. Module 9 covers this in depth.

C. Raise the alarm limit to avoid nuisance trips.

✗ Not quite. That hides the evidence. Investigate the cause first.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Vibration is oscillation about equilibrium, described by amplitude (how far), frequency (how fast) and phase (how points move relative to each other).
  • RMS is the energy-based amplitude used for trending and severity limits. RMS = peak/√2 only for a pure sine wave, and RMS can hide impacts.
  • vpk = ωypk and apk = ω²ypk. Displacement suits low frequencies, velocity the running-speed range, and acceleration high frequencies.
  • Real systems are modelled as m, k and c, using equivalent stiffness (series and parallel) and effective mass.
  • Free vibration occurs at fn = (1/2π)√(k/m) and decays at a rate set by ζ = c/2√(km). Damping is measured with the logarithmic decrement.

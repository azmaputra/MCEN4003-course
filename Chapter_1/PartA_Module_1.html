Introduction

We meet vibration every day. You feel it through the floor of a moving bus, hear it when a phone buzzes on a desk, and see it when a guitar string blurs as it sounds. In engineering, the same phenomenon decides whether a pump runs quietly for decades or cracks its pipework within months. This part shows why vibration matters, then sets out what you will learn in this module and what you need to bring to it.

Start hereWhy does this matter?

A cooling-water pump on a Pilbara processing site has run quietly at 1,480 rpm for two years. Over the last four months, its monthly vibration reading has crept upward: 1.9, 2.6, 3.4, then 4.3 mm/s RMS. Nothing about the pump has changed. It runs at the same speed and flow, with the same impeller.

A technician shuts the pump down and strikes its steel skid once with a rubber mallet. The skid rings, like a bell, and the trace above is what an accelerometer records.

Why would a structure that was fine for two years start to shake, and what is that ring telling us?

🤔Predict first

What do you think is the most likely explanation? Pick one before reading on.

A. The pump bearings are wearing out, so the forcing is growing.

A reasonable first suspect, and engineers do check it. But notice the clue: the skid rings when struck with the pump switched off. Keep this as one hypothesis among several.

B. Something about the skid or its supports has changed.

A good instinct. Keep it as a hypothesis. By the end of this module, you will be able to test it with numbers.

C. The vibration sensor has drifted.

Worth ruling out, but a drifting sensor cannot make a steel frame ring when you hit it. The structure is telling us something too.

D. Nothing is wrong. Vibration always increases slowly with age.

Not so. Healthy machines produce a stable trend. A steady rise is exactly what condition-monitoring programmes are designed to catch.

We return to this pump in the Engineering Challenge in Part I, with measured data. By then you will have the tools to diagnose it.

The ring is the skid's free vibration: the way it moves when disturbed once and then left alone. Every structure has its own preferred frequencies. If a machine pushes it at, or near, one of them, a modest force can produce large motion. To make sense of this, you first need a precise language for describing vibration (amplitude, frequency and phase) and a way to turn a real structure into a model you can calculate.

Vibration costs money, and sometimes lives

Piping vibration in oil and gas

In one Malaysian oil and gas case study, a cracked pipe forced a production module to shut down, costing about US$2 million per day. A six-year piping-vibration programme at the same operator recorded around US$400 million in value.

Structures and wind

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge (Washington, 1940) twisted itself apart in a steady wind. Wind-driven vibration still governs the design of tall buildings, chimneys and long bridges.

People

Whole-body vibration in mobile machinery contributes to back pain. Hand–arm vibration from tools such as jackhammers can cause vibration white finger. Exposure limits are regulated by law.

Noise

Railway rolling noise is radiated by the vibrating wheel and rail. Most machinery noise starts life as structural vibration.

⚠Common Misconception: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge was simple resonance

“Tacoma Narrows collapsed because the wind's vortex shedding matched the bridge's natural frequency.” This explanation is common but incomplete. The final, destructive twisting is now generally attributed to aeroelastic flutter: the deck's own twisting motion changed the airflow, so the bridge was extracting energy from the wind in a self-excited way. Vortex shedding did drive the earlier, milder vertical oscillations.

Why it matters: the fix depends on the mechanism. Flutter is addressed with aerodynamic shape and torsional stiffness. Forced resonance is addressed by separating frequencies or adding damping. Always ask why it is vibrating before deciding how to stop it.

🧭The questions an engineer asks about every vibration problem

  • What is physically vibrating?
  • Why is it vibrating? What is the source of the force or motion?
  • What controls the vibration: mass, stiffness or damping?
  • At what frequency is it vibrating?
  • What does the mathematics tell me physically?
  • What would I measure on the real system?
  • How would I reduce or control the vibration?

Keep these questions in mind throughout this unit. Every part of this module answers at least one of them.

OverviewAbout this module

Every machine and structure vibrates. Most of the time it is harmless; occasionally it cracks pipes, wrecks bearings, shakes buildings and shuts down plants. This module builds the foundations you will use in every later module. OrientationWhat will you learn?

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Describe any vibration using amplitude (peak, peak-to-peak, RMS), frequency (Hz, rad/s, CPM) and phase, and choose the right descriptor for the job.
  • Convert between displacement, velocity and acceleration for a harmonic vibration, and explain which one to measure in a given situation.
  • Interpret what happens when vibrations combine, including beating, and read a simple frequency spectrum.
  • Model a real machine or structure as a mass–spring–damper, including equivalent stiffness and effective mass.
  • Predict free vibration: calculate the natural frequency and damping ratio, and estimate damping from a measured decay.

Before you startWhat do you already know?

This module builds on four things from earlier units. Check each one quickly before you start.

Newton's second law: what is ΣF = ma for a mass on a spring?

If the spring force is \(-kx\), then \(m\ddot{x} = -kx\). The spring always pulls the mass back toward equilibrium. That restoring force is the reason vibration exists at all.

Calculus: what is the derivative of A sin(ωt)?

\(\frac{d}{dt}A\sin\omega t = A\omega\cos\omega t\). Each derivative multiplies the amplitude by \(\omega\). You will use this to link displacement, velocity and acceleration.

Beam theory: how far does a cantilever tip deflect under a tip load F?

\(\delta = \dfrac{FL^3}{3EI}\). Rearranged as \(F/\delta\), this is the beam's stiffness, and you will use it to build vibration models.

Differential equations: what kind of equation is m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = 0?

A linear, second-order, homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients. You solve it with a trial solution est, which gives a characteristic equation.

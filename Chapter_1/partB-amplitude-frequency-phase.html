Describing a vibration: amplitude, frequency and phase

What is vibrating, how severe, and in what pattern.

What is physically vibrating?

Put your hand on a running electric motor and you feel it: a small, rapid back-and-forth motion. Vibration is the oscillation of an object or structure about its equilibrium position. The equilibrium position is where the object would sit if nothing disturbed it.

To describe that motion completely, an engineer needs three answers:

  • How far does it move from equilibrium? This is amplitude.
  • How fast does it repeat? This is frequency.
  • How does it move relative to another point or to a reference? This is phase.

💡Engineering Intuition

Small vibration is normal in every rotating machine, because rotating parts are never perfectly balanced or aligned. The engineering question is never “is it vibrating?” but “is it vibrating more than it should, and why?”

From rotation to a sine wave

🤔Predict first

A crank turns at constant speed and drives a piston up and down. If you plotted the piston's height against time, what shape would you get?

A. A triangle wave: it moves at constant speed up, then down.

Check the turnaround points: does a crank really reverse instantly at the top?

B. A sine wave: smooth, slowest at the top and bottom.

Test it. Watch how fast the mass moves near the extremes compared with the middle.

C. A square wave: it jumps between top and bottom.

Could a physical mass jump instantly? Think about what infinite acceleration would need.

Was your prediction correct? Why did the system behave this way?

The motion is a sine wave. The height of a point on a circle turning at constant angular speed ω is A sin(ωt + φ).

Near the top and bottom, the crank pin is moving mostly sideways, so the yoke and mass slow and reverse smoothly. Through the middle, the pin moves mostly vertically, so the mass is fastest. Changing A changes how far the mass moves but not how often, because amplitude and frequency are independent. Changing φ shifts the whole wave in time without changing its shape. For a single vibration like this one, though, φ has no useful physical meaning: it only depends on the instant you choose to start the clock. Phase becomes meaningful when we compare two motions, as relative phase, later in this part.

The harmonic vibration

$$y(t) = A\,\sin(\omega t + \phi) \tag{1.1}$$

What does this equation tell us?

  • \(y(t)\): the instantaneous displacement from equilibrium.
  • \(A\): the peak displacement, or amplitude. It sets how far the object moves.
  • \(\omega\): the angular frequency in rad/s. It sets how fast the motion repeats.
  • \(\phi\): the phase in radians. It sets where in its cycle the motion is at \(t = 0\).

Engineering significance: every steady vibration, however complicated, can be built from sine waves like this one. That idea underpins every spectrum you will see in this unit.

The angular frequency is linked to quantities you can see on a time waveform: the period T (seconds per cycle) and the frequency f (cycles per second, in hertz).

$$\begin{aligned} T &= \frac{2\pi}{\omega} &&\quad \text{(s)} \\[6pt] f &= \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} &&\quad \text{(Hz)} \\[6pt] \omega &= 2\pi f &&\quad \text{(rad/s)} \end{aligned} \tag{1.2}$$

What does this equation tell us?

One full cycle is \(2\pi\) radians of rotation. If the shaft turns faster (larger \(\omega\)), each cycle takes less time, so T falls and f rises.

Rotating machinery is usually quoted in rpm (revolutions per minute) or CPM (cycles per minute), because it ties vibration directly to shaft speed. To convert, divide by 60. A pump at 600 CPM vibrates at \(600/60 = 10\) Hz, so \(\omega = 2\pi \times 10 = 62.8\) rad/s.

💡Engineering Intuition

Frequency is the fingerprint of a vibration source. A component that repeats once per shaft revolution (at “1×” running speed) points toward the rotor. Components at other multiples point elsewhere: blade-pass, gear-mesh, electrical line frequency. Chapters 9–12 are largely about reading those fingerprints.

⚠Common Misconception: “A bigger vibration is a faster vibration”

Amplitude and frequency are independent. A large, slow sway of a tall building and a tiny, fast buzz of a gearbox are both vibration, at very different A and f. In the simulation, reducing A left the period unchanged.

Phase: how points move relative to each other

Measure two feet of the same motor and you may find identical amplitude and frequency, yet the motor could be doing two completely different things. The extra piece of information is the phase difference Δφ between the two signals.

🤔Predict first

Two accelerometers on foot A and foot B of a motor show the same amplitude and frequency. When A is at its highest, B is at its lowest. What is the motor doing?

A. Bouncing straight up and down.

If the motor were bouncing, would the two feet ever be at opposite extremes at the same moment?

B. Rocking about a point between the feet.

Test it. Set Δφ = 180° and watch the plate.

C. Not moving. The two signals cancel out.

Signals from different points do not cancel. Each foot really is moving.

Was your prediction correct? Why did the system behave this way?

At Δφ = 180°, the motor rocks. At 0°, it bounces. In between, the motion is a combination of the two.

Phase tells you the shape of the motion, not just its size. A rocking motor stresses its hold-down bolts and foundation very differently from a bouncing one. When B's amplitude is zero, B is effectively the pivot. Unequal amplitudes move the pivot off-centre.

The same idea applies to continuous structures. A cantilever beam can vibrate in different mode shapes. Comparing the phase at two points tells you which shape it is in.

Phase on a vibrating cantilever

Learning question: In each mode shape, do point 1 and the tip move together or in opposite directions? What changes when point 1 moves past the node?

Was your prediction correct? Why did the system behave this way?

In mode 1, the whole beam bends to the same side at every instant, so every point is in phase with the tip. Only the amplitude changes along the beam, growing from zero at the clamp.

In mode 2, the beam has a node at about 0.78L, a point that does not move. Points on opposite sides of a node always move in opposite directions, so they are 180° out of phase. Move point 1 past the node and it is back on the tip's side, so the phase returns to 0°.

This is why phase measured at several points reveals the mode shape: a 180° jump between neighbouring points tells you a node lies between them.

💡Engineering Intuition

Amplitude at one point tells you how much. Phase between many points tells you the shape. Often an engineer needs that shape to find the problem: a support with weak stiffness, a loose bolt, a cracked weld or a soft foot shows up as a point that moves much more than its neighbours, or out of step with them. Measure amplitude and phase at enough points on a running machine and software can animate its actual motion. This technique is called Operating Deflection Shape (ODS) analysis, covered in Module 8.

Putting it together: amplitude, frequency and phase on a fan

Learning question: What changes in the motion, and in the graph, when you change A, f or the phase difference Δφ between the two mounts, one at a time?

What did you notice?

Amplitude A changes how far the fan moves. The wave gets taller, but the peaks stay in the same places in time.

Frequency f changes how often the motion repeats. More cycles fit in the window and the period T = 1/f gets shorter, but the height does not change. Because the heavy spot goes round once per revolution, the fan vibrates at its shaft speed. This is called the 1× frequency.

Phase difference Δφ changes how the two mounts move relative to each other, and so the shape of the motion. At 0°, both mounts move together and the fan bounces. At 180°, one is up while the other is down and the fan rocks. In between, it does both. Both mounts still have the same amplitude and frequency: only phase can tell these motions apart.

Answer in your head or on paper first. Then open the feedback.

Q1. A fan runs at 1,800 CPM. What is its rotational frequency?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. 1,800 Hz

✗ Not quite. CPM means cycles per minute. Divide by 60 to get cycles per second.

B. 30 Hz, which is 188.5 rad/s

✓ Correct. 1,800/60 = 30 Hz, and ω = 2π × 30 = 188.5 rad/s. Both describe the same speed in different units.

C. 30 rad/s

✗ Not quite. 30 is the frequency in Hz (cycles per second). One cycle is 2π radians, so ω = 2π × 30 = 188.5 rad/s.

D. 286 Hz

✗ Not quite. That is 1,800/2π, which mixes up the conversions. CPM to Hz is a division by 60. Hz to rad/s is a multiplication by 2π.

Q2. Two points on a vibrating cantilever beam are 180° out of phase. What can you conclude?

Choose an answer to open its feedback.

A. The beam is vibrating in its first mode.

✗ Not quite. In mode 1, every point on a cantilever moves to the same side together, so all points are in phase.

B. There is at least one node between the two points.

✓ Correct. To move in opposite directions, the deflected shape must cross the undeformed line somewhere between them. That crossing is a node.

C. The two points have different frequencies.

✗ Not quite. Phase difference is only meaningful when both signals have the same frequency. Here they do; they are just shifted in time.

Q3. A colleague says: “Both bearings on this fan read 4 mm/s at 1×, so the vibration is the same at both ends.” What extra measurement would you request, and why?

Compare with the model answer

Request the phase between the two bearings at 1× running speed. Equal amplitudes at 0° suggest the rotor is translating (a classic static-unbalance pattern). Equal amplitudes at about 180° suggest it is rocking (a couple-unbalance pattern). Amplitude alone cannot tell these apart, but the corrections are different. You will see this again in Module 8.
